The Evolution of Computing: From Brass Gears to the World Wide Web Chapter 1: The Mechanical Spark (1830s) Long before silicon chips or electronic screens existed, the story of computing began with Charles Babbage in 19th-century England. The Analytical Engine: Known as the "Father of Computing," Babbage designed a massive, steam-powered mechanical machine made of brass gears and punch cards. The Vision: Though never fully built in his lifetime, Babbage’s design included the fundamental concepts of modern computers: an arithmetic unit (the "Mill"), memory (the "Store"), and programmable input/output using punch cards. Ada Lovelace wrote the world's first algorithm for this machine. Chapter 2: Hardware Generations & The Document Dilemma (1940s – 1970s) As electronics replaced mechanical gears, computers evolved through hardware generations: 1st Generation (1940s – 1950s): Powered by bulky, fragile vacuum tubes (e.g., ENIAC). 2nd Generation (1950s – 1960s): Transistors replaced vacuum tubes, making computers smaller and faster. 3rd Generation (1960s – 1970s): Integrated Circuits (ICs) allowed thousands of components on a single chip. 4th Generation (1970s–Present): Microprocessors brought personal computers (PCs) into businesses and homes. The Document & Military Connection (GML & SGML) In the 1960s and 1970s, military organizations, publishers, and major corporations faced a huge problem: every computer brand used different file formats. If a military contractor typed a user manual on one system, another system couldn't open or print it. GML (Generalized Markup Language): In 1969, Charles Goldfarb, Edward Mosher, and Raymond Lorie at IBM created GML. Instead of specifying how text should look on a specific screen, GML tagged document elements structurally (e.g., :h1. for headers, :p. for paragraphs). SGML (Standard Generalized Markup Language): GML evolved into SGML, which became the standard used by the U.S. Department of Defense and major industries to archive technical documents safely across different machines. Chapter 3: Connecting Computers — IP Addresses & DNS (1960s–1980s) Computers were still isolated islands. To transmit files between separate computers, the U.S. Department of Defense (DARPA) created ARPANET (the precursor to the Internet). To send data packets to the correct machine, two key systems were created: IP Address (Internet Protocol Address): Every computer on the network was assigned a unique numerical identity (like 192.168.1.1 or 142.250.190.46). DNS (Domain Name System): Humans couldn't easily memorize long strings of numbers for every computer. In 1983, DNS was introduced as the "phonebook of the Internet." It translates human-readable domain names like google.com into the raw numerical IP address (142.250.190.46). Chapter 4: The Birth of HTML & The Web (1989–1990) By the late 1980s, the Internet existed, but it was difficult to navigate and primarily used by technical researchers typing command-line prompts. The Problem at CERN At CERN (the European Organization for Nuclear Research in Switzerland), thousands of scientists from around the world brought different computers and file formats. Valuable research papers and documents were trapped on individual systems. Tim Berners-Lee’s Breakthrough In 1989, British scientist Tim Berners-Lee proposed a solution: The World Wide Web. Main Purpose: To upload, organize, link, and transmit scientific research papers across different computers effortlessly. HTML (HyperText Markup Language): Tim took the heavy, complex SGML standard and simplified it into HTML. By wrapping text in tags (hyperlinks), documents could link directly to other documents hosted on entirely different computers. Chapter 5: Browsers, Protocols, and the Modern URL (1990s) To make HTML documents work across the Internet, three core elements were established: 1. HTTP & HTTPS (The Protocols) HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol): The set of rules computers use to request and send HTML documents over the network. HTTPS (HTTP Secure): Added an encryption layer (SSL/TLS) to keep passwords, bank details, and personal data secure from interception. 2. The First Browser & The Microsoft Era The First Web Browser (1990): Tim Berners-Lee created the very first web browser, named WorldWideWeb (later renamed Nexus). Mosaic & Netscape (1993–1994): Marc Andreessen built Mosaic (and later Netscape Navigator), introducing graphics, images, and user-friendly buttons to the web. Microsoft & The Web (1995+): Microsoft realized the Internet was the future and bundled Internet Explorer into Windows 95, sparking the "First Browser War" and making web browsing accessible to millions of everyday household users worldwide.
